research

Parallel programming models, runtime systems, and research computing.

My research focuses on programming models, languages, and runtime systems for high-performance parallel computing. I am especially interested in irregular applications, which use dynamic, unbalanced workloads or globally shared, pointer-based data structures such as trees and graphs, on distributed-memory systems that have no physically shared memory. Most of my current work is done with Rhodes undergraduates.

Network-accelerated dynamic load balancing

Work stealing is a simple and effective way to balance irregular, dynamically generated work across a parallel machine. On distributed-memory systems, however, every steal costs several round trips over the network. My recent work uses the offload capabilities of modern high-performance networks to take the CPU out of that loop. It re-purposes message matching, triggered operations, and network atomic operations so that steals require less communication.

  • Structured atomic operations in OpenSHMEM. ICPP ‘21, with Cameron Cartier† and James Dinan. Code: SAWS.
  • Accelerated work stealing with Portals 4. ICPP ‘19, with John Snyder† and James Dinan. Code: PAWS.
  • Locality-aware work stealing. Kamil Yousuf, Rhodes Undergraduate Research Symposium, 2024.

Partitioned global logical address spaces

Partitioned global address space (PGAS) models give programmers a shared view of distributed data, but they tie each piece of data to a physical location. A logical address space lets the runtime and network hardware locate data by key, much like a distributed hash table, which makes irregular data structures easier to build and lets the network do more of the work.

  • COMHPC ‘16 and ICPP ‘18, with John Snyder† and James Dinan. Code: PDHT.

Global-view programming of irregular data structures

My graduate work at Ohio State, advised by P. Sadayappan and in collaboration with colleagues at Pacific Northwest National Laboratory, focused on runtime support for global-view programming. My current work builds on it.

  • Global Trees (my dissertation work): a framework for linked data structures on distributed-memory parallel systems. SC ‘08. See also ACMSE ‘12 on data locality for irregular PGAS programs.
  • Scioto (with James Dinan): global-view task parallelism with scalable work stealing. ICPP ‘08 and SC ‘09.

At Ohio State, I also worked on Brew, a language and compiler with multiple dispatch, strong code mobility, and retroactive data abstraction, and on the Tensor Contraction Engine, a domain-specific compiler that generates highly tuned Fortran for tensor contraction expressions.

Research computing at Rhodes

As principal investigator on the NSF award CC* Compute: A High Performance Computing Cluster to Accelerate Research, Education and Training at Rhodes College (Award #2018758, $389,662), I brought high-performance computing to Rhodes. The resulting cluster, lotus, is housed in Briggs Hall and supports both high-performance and high-throughput workloads as a shared, campus-wide research computing resource. It is also used in my parallel computing course.

  • Compute: 44 compute nodes with 2,112 cores
  • Memory: 11.6 TB
  • Storage: 350 TB

  • Interconnect: 100 Gb/s InfiniBand
